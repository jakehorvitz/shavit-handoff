The story

Question, evidence, people. No stages.

The new spine is not a timeline. Rev 2 asked the viewer to track five stages of a job they have never seen, which is why you kept asking how we got to a step. Rev 3 asks a question, buries the viewer in evidence, then answers with faces. There is nothing to follow and nothing to lose.

Two cards open it, in your words: Building houses is hard. Then Ever wonder how it actually happens? What follows is eleven frames of real mess, and the only on screen text is your own subtext idea, Building South Norwood Street, Building Barry Street, Building Howder Street. Same verb every time. That repetition is the whole argument: this is not a portfolio, this is a Tuesday.

The captivating part is the escalation. The frames are not in job order, they are in intensity order. It opens quiet, a pallet of material sitting on the front steps, and it ends loud, a man in a hi-vis vest standing in a house with its roof open to the sky. The cuts get shorter as it goes. By the time the black card asks how we do it, the viewer has just watched a crew and wants the names.

And because every frame is a jobsite, nothing is awkward. No finished kitchen interrupts a demolition. That was the actual defect.

Transitions and motion

How this thing actually moves

The rule for the whole reel is that movement is decisive and it settles. Nothing drifts. Carried forward from rev 2, with the escalation rules added.

  • The anchored push, not Ken Burns. Ken Burns is a slow linear crawl across a still for the whole duration, and it reads as filler. Every push here is short, eased out, and anchored on the subject: it starts moving, decelerates, and comes to rest before the cut. Range is 2 to 4 percent, never more.
  • Motion escalates with the cut length. Block 1 is mostly static and holds 2.0 to 2.2s. Block 3 runs 1.6s with pushes on nearly every frame. The reel physically speeds up without a single speed ramp.
  • One handheld frame only, A10, the crew. Two pixels of wobble. It is the only frame that breaks the locked off grammar, and it breaks it exactly where a person appears.
  • Cuts land on the beat, hard, no dissolves anywhere in section A.
  • Property blocks change on a texture match. Norwood ends on torn plaster, Barry opens on torn tile. Barry ends on a laid floor, Howder opens on a floor pulled up. Same surface, opposite state, so the block change feels like a cut inside one job rather than a jump to a new address.
  • The progress rule. A two pixel brass line sits at the very bottom of frame and fills left to right across the whole of section A, empty at the first card and full on the last framing frame. Almost subliminal, and it is what makes three addresses read as one continuous run.
  • Hard cut to black into the question card. No dissolve, no fade. The silence of the cut is what makes the question land.
  • Team block settle. Each portrait enters at 104 percent and settles to 100 over 10 frames, with the name fading up 4 frames after the image lands, so the face registers before the text does.
  • The outro does not move. The plate holds dead still for the full 3 seconds.

Higgsfield: tested tonight, and here is exactly where it works

Two real generations against the A5 Norwood still, 15 credits spent, 77.5 remaining. Preflight was walked in full before either. Findings, evidenced by extracted frames in review/motion-A5_0.jpg and review/motion-A5v2_0.jpg:

  • A five second dolly-in fails. By roughly 3.5s the black contractor bag and the broom dissolve, the R13 bag relocates onto the stud wall, and the framing layout changes. The camera travels past what the still can support and the model invents. That is checklist 1.3 and 1.4.
  • A restrained push holds for about two seconds. Verified at 0s, 1s, 2s and 2.5s: every object stays put, the move is real and gentle. Degradation begins after that.
  • Two seconds is exactly what this reel needs, because every section A hold is 1.6 to 2.2s. So the working recipe is: generate five seconds, keep the first two, throw the rest away.

Delivered: motion/A5-norwood-framing-TRIM2s.mp4, a real 2.0s camera move on the real photo, nothing invented. Source crop is raw/pick3/14-norwood-framing-916.jpg.

The honest caveat: for a 2 to 4 percent anchored push, a deterministic transform in the edit gives the identical result for zero credits and zero invention risk. Higgsfield earns its place on the bigger moves, and the bigger the move the more it breaks. Recommend generative motion only where the move genuinely exceeds what a transform can do, for example the rafters parallax at A9.
